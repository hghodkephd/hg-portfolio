Affinity and complex stability scoring

Which candidates form the most credible modeled complexes — and do the scoring methods agree?

PRODIGY OpenMM MDTraj Boltz-2 BioPython Shrake-Rupley BSA Contact persistence
These scores are not experimental affinities. They are comparative triage features — different lenses on modeled complex quality. The output is a prioritized shortlist of hypotheses, not validated binders.

The scoring problem

Twenty-eight designs survived the full computational pipeline: RFdiffusion backbone generation, ProteinMPNN sequence design, ESMFold fold validation, structural filtering, Boltz-2 complex prediction, interface fingerprinting, and 1 ns molecular dynamics simulations. Each design now has metrics from multiple independent analyses. The question is whether these metrics tell a consistent story — and what happens when they don't.

Five distinct evidence types were assembled for each design. Boltz-2 complex confidence (ipTM) asks whether an independent structure predictor recovers a plausible complex from sequence alone. Static interface geometry — buried surface area, residue contact counts, and polar close-contacts — describes the physical extent of the modeled interface after energy minimization. PRODIGY provides an empirical affinity proxy trained on experimentally characterized natural complexes. MD-derived stability metrics — binder backbone RMSD, center-of-mass drift, and interfacial contact counts — test whether the complex holds together during short simulation. And post-MD contact persistence measures which designed contacts survive dynamics.

Cross-stage agreement is limited

If the pipeline stages were measuring the same underlying property, their outputs would be strongly correlated. They are not. Most cross-stage relationships are weak or absent, which means each stage is contributing genuinely independent information.

The non-correlations are as informative as the correlations. Each pipeline stage filters on a different failure mode: sequence foldability, complex plausibility, interface extent, affinity-like scoring, and dynamic coherence. This is why a multi-stage workflow adds value — no single metric captures the full picture.

Static contacts predict dynamic persistence

The strongest mechanistic finding from this analysis: designs that start with more residue-level contacts in the minimized structure tend to retain more contacts through molecular dynamics. The Spearman correlation (ρ = +0.679, p = 0.0001) suggests that static interface fingerprinting — a computationally cheap step — has real predictive value for dynamic stability. The interface geometry is not merely an artifact of threading and minimization; it reflects something about how well the designed sequence encodes contacts with the target.

Static interface contacts vs. post-MD contact persistence (n = 28)

This partially validates the earlier pipeline stages as informative filters rather than noise. Designs that pass the static fingerprinting screen with strong contact networks are more likely to retain those contacts under thermal stress.

PRODIGY tracks interface size

PRODIGY's empirical model predicts binding free energy from interfacial contact features. In this dataset, PRODIGY ΔG correlates very strongly with buried surface area (ρ = +0.825). This is a useful sanity check — and a practical finding: PRODIGY is not providing much independent information beyond what BSA and contact counts already capture. For triage purposes, the simpler geometric metrics may be sufficient.

Buried surface area vs. PRODIGY ΔG (n = 28)

The PRODIGY ΔG range across the 28 designs spans −5.2 to −11.4 kcal/mol (mean −8.2 ± 1.8). These values are plausible for protein–protein interfaces of this size, but the model was trained on natural complexes — its calibration for de novo designs is unknown. The relative ranking is more trustworthy than the absolute numbers.

Consensus ranking: the downstream scorecard

A composite score was constructed from rank-normalized metrics across static interface geometry, PRODIGY affinity, MD stability, and contact persistence. All 28 designs received "high" confidence classification because all evidence sources were available.

The compact outlier

A 25-residue clusterA design (len25_clusterA_noise0__design_15_0_rank0) reaches consensus rank 2 despite having a modest BSA (487 Å²) and only moderate PRODIGY ΔG (−5.15 kcal/mol). Its strength comes from low binder RMSD (1.54 Å) and tight COM drift. A 25-residue binder that holds its fold and stays engaged is noteworthy — it competes on dynamic stability despite a limited contact footprint. This is the kind of candidate that would be lost if the pipeline used only interface-size metrics as gates.

The disagreement between these two rankings is itself informative. The consensus scorecard weights downstream evidence (MD, persistence) equally with upstream evidence; the pipeline composite averages across all stages. Different weighting schemes answer different questions, and no single ranking should be treated as definitive.

What this means

Structural screening narrows the field, but the surviving designs remain supported by different combinations of evidence. The output of this analysis is a shortlist, not a declared winner. The shortlist preserves diversity across consensus performance, interface geometry, dynamic persistence, compact architecture, hotspot strategy, and binder length — because the strongest experimental panel tests multiple hypotheses, not one.
