AI-Guided Protein Design · Volume 1

A Wet-Lab Scientist's Guide to AI-Enabled Protein Design

From a target structure to a ranked experimental shortlist: an end-to-end campaign for designing de novo mini-protein binders against PD-L1.

Generative protein-design tools can produce convincing structures in minutes. The harder problem is deciding which outputs are credible, which analyses are informative, and which candidates are worth carrying into the laboratory.

This portfolio documents that decision process through a complete illustrative protein-design campaign. The objective is not simply to generate a binder. It is to construct a defensible screening funnel that moves from a large computational design space toward a small, experimentally actionable set of candidates.

Volume 1 follows the design of de novo mini-protein binders against PD-L1. The campaign begins with target selection and design strategy, then moves through backbone generation, sequence design, fold recovery, interface analysis, molecular dynamics, independent complex prediction, and consensus ranking.

At each stage, the emphasis is on the questions a campaign lead must answer: What should be trusted? What should be filtered out? When do two models provide meaningful independent support? Which uncertainties can be reduced computationally, and which must ultimately be resolved by experiment?

Protein engineering campaigns run on DBTL cycles

Protein engineering is the deliberate modification or creation of proteins to achieve a useful function. A campaign may begin with an existing natural protein that needs to be improved, a known scaffold that can be retargeted, or — as in this volume — a desired function with no starting binder at all.

The goal is rarely to optimize a single property. A successful molecule must usually balance activity or binding with specificity, stability, expression, solubility, and manufacturability. Protein engineering is therefore a structured search through a large design space for molecules that satisfy several competing requirements at once.

A campaign coordinates repeated rounds of design, construction, testing, and analysis to develop a molecule against a defined performance specification. Unlike a single experiment, a campaign has explicit budgets, timelines, decision gates, and stopping criteria. The organizing principle is the Design-Build-Test-Learn cycle:

🔵 Design

Propose candidate molecules using computational methods. This volume covers the design phase in depth.

🟢 Build

Synthesize DNA, express protein, and purify candidates. Many designs fail here. The build phase is a filter, not merely manufacturing.

🟡 Test

Run binding, stability, and functional assays. Each assay should answer a defined question using appropriate controls.

🟣 Learn

Analyze outcomes and feed structured results into the next round. This is often the least developed phase of a campaign.

Figure 1. The DBTL cycle. Each round passes through all four phases, with structured outcomes from the Learn phase informing the next Design round. This volume examines the computational Design phase in depth; the concluding module connects candidate prioritization to Build, Test, and Learn.

In an academic study, a successful protein design may be treated as the endpoint. In a campaign, the result of Round 1 is the input to Round 2. Designs that fail can be as informative as those that succeed, but only when sequences, design conditions, assay results, and failure modes are captured systematically. Structured failure analysis is part of the campaign, not an afterthought.

The working codebase behind this portfolio

The accompanying campaign guide provides a complete, end-to-end codebase for computational mini-binder design: cleaned and annotated Colab notebooks, helper scripts, example inputs and outputs, troubleshooting notes, and a consistent data structure connecting each stage of the campaign.

What you get

Notebooks Annotated Colab notebooks covering every pipeline stage, from target preparation through candidate scoring
Data structure A consistent project layout connecting upstream outputs to downstream inputs
Retargetable Supply a new target structure and binding surface, then use the same infrastructure to generate, triage, simulate, and prioritize candidates
Troubleshooting Common failure modes and practical workarounds documented where they occur

Who this is for

Scientists who want to apply existing protein-design models and translate their outputs into experimental decisions. The guide teaches campaign execution and scientific judgment rather than neural-network architecture or model training. It is particularly useful for researchers moving into computational design from a wet-lab background.

The individual tools used in this workflow are publicly available. The guide provides the integration layer: a tested and consistent route from target preparation to candidate prioritization, so that time can be spent interpreting the science rather than debugging file paths and model handoffs.

The objective is not to provide a pipeline to run blindly. The objective is to develop the judgment required to design a new campaign for a different target, modality, or experimental constraint and to make defensible decisions at every stage. That judgment is what separates someone who runs tools from someone who runs campaigns.
