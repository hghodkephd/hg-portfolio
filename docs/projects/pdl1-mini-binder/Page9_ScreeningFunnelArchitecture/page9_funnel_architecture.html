How do you architect a screening funnel?

Using multiple imperfect models to allocate resources, preserve informative diversity, and generate the experimental data needed to determine which predictions are actually useful.

Retrospective synthesis Decision architecture DBTL cycle Evidence families Experimental design

The naive model breaks down

The straightforward approach to computational protein design is: generate many candidates, calculate many scores, test the highest-ranked. This campaign shows why that model is insufficient. The tools disagree — ESMFold recovery tells you nothing about Boltz-2 complex confidence (ρ = −0.004). Some metrics are redundant — PRODIGY ΔG is largely recapitulating buried surface area (ρ = +0.825). Rankings depend on weighting — the NB11 consensus scorecard and the pipeline retrospective composite produce different leaders from the same data. Model calibration may not transfer — Prot2Prop was trained on natural proteins, not de novo mini-binders.

The problem is not scoring. The problem is decision architecture: how to convert multiple imperfect and partially independent signals into an intelligent allocation of experimental resources.

Notice that the developability screen runs on all 55 designs, not just the 28 that survived to MD. Sequence-level models are cheap enough to apply early and broadly. A revised funnel might move Prot2Prop earlier — flagging probable expression failures before investing in Boltz-2 or MD — though this depends on whether its predictions prove accurate for de novo designs.

Separate filters from prioritization metrics

Filters remove candidates that fail minimum viability requirements: a backbone that does not recapitulate in ESMFold, a sequence that fails to form any interface contacts, a Boltz-2 prediction with an alternative binding pose. These are hard gates. Prioritization metrics help choose among surviving candidates: PRODIGY ΔG, MD persistence, predicted solubility. These should remain continuous ranking signals, not become additional gates, unless experimentally calibrated for the relevant protein class.

The distinction matters because a strict gate on a prioritization metric will eliminate unusual but informative candidates. The 25-residue binder at consensus rank 2 has modest BSA (487 Å²) — a BSA gate at 500 Å² would have killed it despite its excellent dynamic stability.

Group metrics by biological question

Not every column in a scoring matrix should act as an independent vote. Metrics that answer the same biological question belong to the same evidence family. Giving each family equal voice — rather than each column — prevents accidental over-weighting of categories that contain more individual metrics.

Two evidence families contain internal redundancies. The affinity proxy (PRODIGY) is largely recapitulating interface geometry (BSA), so these might be merged into a single "interface quality" family. Contact persistence correlates with static contacts — they address the same question at different timescales. Recognizing these overlaps prevents double-counting.

Select a panel, not merely the top N

An intelligent experimental panel tests multiple hypotheses, not one. The best learning set is not necessarily the top ten rows of a composite leaderboard — it is a set of candidates that spans the informative diversity of the design space and allows the experimental results to discriminate which computational signals actually matter.

Consensus candidates
Strong across most evidence families. The safest bets if the pipeline is well-calibrated.
Tests whether computational consensus translates to experimental success.
Interface-forward candidates
High BSA, many contacts, strong PRODIGY ΔG — but possibly lower developability.
Tests whether interface extent drives binding, even at the cost of monomer producibility.
Dynamics-forward candidates
Low binder RMSD, minimal COM drift, high contact persistence under MD.
Tests whether MD stability predicts experimental binding better than static scoring.
Developability-forward candidates
Favorable production, solubility, and aggregation predictions, even if structural scores are moderate.
Tests whether producibility is the binding constraint — designs that do not express cannot bind.
Compact or unusual candidates
Short binders (25 aa), unconventional hotspot strategies, or architectures underrepresented in the top composite ranks.
Tests whether the pipeline's preference for longer, interface-heavy designs reflects a real physics advantage or a scoring bias.
Borderline or negative controls
Candidates near computational thresholds, selected to calibrate where the pipeline's discrimination breaks down.
Without borderline cases, every success confirms the pipeline and every failure is uninformative.

Close the design–build–test–learn loop

The computational pipeline produces hypotheses. Experiments test them. Structured data preserves the context. A feedback model guides the next round. This is not a one-pass process — it is a loop, and the critical next step is experimental calibration.

The retrospective should ask: which observables predict expression? Which predict binding success? Which predict affinity among confirmed binders? Does MD add information beyond static contacts? Does Boltz-2 confidence add information beyond interface size? Which metrics are redundant? Which tools should move earlier or later in the next funnel? Which outputs should be removed because they add cost without predictive value?

What we observed in this campaign

The cross-stage correlation analysis revealed a specific pattern of independence and redundancy that shapes the funnel architecture.

Three largely independent evidence families emerged

Fold quality — ProteinMPNN score, ESMFold pLDDT, ESMFold RMSD. Does the sequence encode a well-folded monomer? Weakly correlated with everything downstream.

Interface engagement — BSA, contacts, PRODIGY ΔG, MD persistence, Boltz-2 ipTM. Does the binder make and maintain contact with PD-L1? Internally correlated (BSA↔PRODIGY, contacts↔persistence) but largely independent of fold quality and developability.

Monomer developability — Prot2Prop solubility, expression, folding, thermostability, aggregation, production. Will the protein actually produce and behave? Mostly independent of the structural pipeline, with one notable anticorrelation: production probability vs. interface contacts (ρ = −0.530).

The low cross-correlations between these families mean they filter on different failure modes. A design that passes all three has survived independent challenges. That is the strongest case for experimental testing — not because any individual score is trustworthy, but because agreement across orthogonal methods reduces the odds of chasing a computational artifact.

Avoid false precision from a master score

Composite scores are useful for exploration but they embed choices: which observables are included, how each is transformed, how missing values are handled, whether correlated metrics are counted repeatedly, how many columns represent each evidence family. The NB11 consensus scorecard and the NB12 augmented pipeline composite produce different top-10 lists from the same underlying data. Neither is wrong — they answer different questions with different implicit priorities.

The score should be treated as a decision aid, not an objective truth. The ranking that matters is the one that produces the most informative experimental panel — and that judgment requires domain knowledge that no composite formula encodes automatically.

What this means

A screening funnel should not be designed to make computational predictions look decisive. It should use imperfect models to allocate resources, preserve informative diversity, and generate the experimental data needed to determine which predictions are actually useful. The objective is not a more elaborate ranking — it is a closed design–build–test–learn system in which each campaign improves the architecture of the next.
